\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The exact result of this paper is the frozen-line solution of section~\ref{sec:frozen}. With an ideal induction equation, smooth fields and no spatial dissipation of the compressive distribution, every volume-integrated single-point statistic, including $W(m)$ and the field-aligned Hermite flux, evolves as in linear phase mixing along the initial field lines, so such dynamics produces no stochastic echoes in $W(m)$.

The two-channel model extends this result to turbulence, where these conditions fail, by treating the spatial dissipation as a sink that removes free energy from every Hermite moment alike, while phase mixing proceeds at the injected field-aligned wavenumber. Its independent content, the relation between the flux and the spectrum with no return flux and the frozen field-aligned spectrum of the free energy, holds in our runs from $64^3$ to $256^3$ grid points. In our runs, then, the relabelling of field lines that \citet{Meyrand2019} invoke does not give $G$ the parallel structure of the Alfv\'enic fields.

Our spectrum at $\beta_i=1$ differs from that of \citet{Meyrand2019}, who found $W(m)$ somewhat steeper than $m^{-1}$ at the same $\beta_i$ and a net flux to high $m$ much smaller than the injection rate. Their set-up differs from ours in four ways that matter here. They forced the compressive fluctuations at $m=1$, the parallel flow, on modes with $|k_x|,|k_y|,|k_z|\le2$, where we force $g_0$ at $|k_z|=k_1$; they damped $G$ with $\mu k_\perp^8$ and $\nu m^6$, where we use a fourth power of $k_\perp$ normalised to the grid and $\nu(m/M)^6$; they ran at $256^3$ to $512^3$ with $M=32$ to 128, against our runs at $\beta_i=1$ on $64^3$ and $128^3$ with $M=32$ and 64; and their Hermite flux is resolved in $k_\perp$ shells, which only in its sum over shells is a pure flux in $m$. In our model, forcing up to $|k_z|=2k_1$ raises the injected $\kappa$ and with it the break $m_*=(s\kappa T)^2/2$, which flattens the spectrum. We tested the forcing directly. With $g_1$ forced on $|k_z|=k_1$ and $2k_1$ at $64^3$, $W(m)$ is nowhere close to $m^{-1}$, and the model with the injected $P(\kappa)$ reproduces it to within 3.5\%, with no return flux (section~\ref{sim:mf1}). The forcing at $m=1$ therefore does not produce their spectrum. Nor does our trend with resolution move towards their results; it moves away from them. At $\beta_i=0.3$, going from $64^3$ to $256^3$ lengthens $T$, raises $W(m)$ at $m\le16$ by up to 43\% and moves the break out (section~\ref{sim:resolution}), which flattens the spectrum. The net field-aligned flux at $m=16$ carries 75\% of the injection in the run with $g_1$ forced, and it grows with the resolution, from 47\% at $64^3$ to 67\% at $256^3$ at $\beta_i=0.3$, and from 66\% at $64^3$ to 73\% at $128^3$ at $\beta_i=1$. The comparison with their small net flux is not direct, since theirs is resolved in $k_\perp$ shells, but ours shows no sign of falling as the resolution increases. The difference therefore lies in the dissipation of $G$ or at resolutions beyond ours, which our runs do not test. Their evidence that $G$ inherits the parallel cascade of the Alfv\'enic fields comes from tracing the perturbed field lines (their figure~3). The parallel spectra of the density and field-strength fluctuations along the lines track that of the Alfv\'enic velocity, and the parallel coherence length of the compressive fluctuations grows with their perpendicular one as that of the Alfv\'enic fluctuations does. In our runs the first does not hold. Along the same lines the spectrum of the density moment stays at the injected wavenumbers, with 0.18\% of its variance beyond $4\,k_1$ at $128^3$ against 3.2\% of the energy of the velocity (section~\ref{sim:kpar}).

 The second compares the fields at a given perpendicular separation, a scale-by-scale measure like the band-resolved wavenumber of section~\ref{sim:kpar}, which gives a copy of $g_0$ frozen to the field lines by construction the same growth with $k_\perp$ as $g_0$ itself. Running our diagnostics, including the frozen copy, on their set-up would show whether the difference lies in the set-up or in the measurement.

The remaining results are measurements at $64^3$, and they describe our runs rather than the plasma. In them the model accounts for how the break moves with $\beta_i$. The trend that \citet{Kawazura2019,Kawazura2020} found in gyrokinetic simulations, $m^{-1/2}$ at high $\beta_i$ and close to $m^{-1}$ at $\beta_i=0.1$, is the trend the model gives without any echo, because $m_*$ falls with $\beta_i$, and a spread of $\kappa$ makes $W(m)$ look like a power law somewhat steeper than $m^{-1}$ near the break (section~\ref{tc:channels}). How far above $\beta_i$ the break lies depends on $T$, and in our runs $T$ depends on the dissipation. Its size in outer eddy-turnover times grows with the resolution, its growth at low $\chi$ comes from runs that also change $\eta$, and the dependence of the sink on the age of the free energy comes from the dissipation range of these runs. Our runs establish none of these as a property of the plasma.

The main open question is the limit of high resolution. The model needs a perpendicular cascade whose rate does not depend on the dissipation coefficients and field lines that stay frozen for the free energy that phase mixes. \citet{DrivasEyink2017} and \citet{Eyink2018} give reasons to expect that the first requires the failure of the second at small enough scales (section~\ref{tc:limits}). Our runs are not converged in the perpendicular resolution. Up to $256^3$ grid points $T$ grows by about the same amount with each doubling of the resolution, from 13 to 25 outer eddy-turnover times at $\beta_i=0.3$ (section~\ref{sim:resolution}), while the field-aligned spectrum of the free energy that phase mixes does not change (section~\ref{sim:kpar}). This is consistent with that argument, but because $T$ has not converged it does not test it. Either the dissipation range of our runs reaches almost to the forcing scales and makes the sink artificially fast, in which case $T$ levels off once the inertial range is long enough and the argument implies that the field lines stop being frozen at the scales where the sink acts. Or the perpendicular cascade of the free energy has no dissipative anomaly, in which case $T$, and with it $m_*$, keeps growing as the dissipation weakens, and the perpendicular cascade loses ground to phase mixing. Deciding between the two needs higher resolution than we reach.

The results hold for $k_\perp\rho_i\to0$. At finite $k_\perp\rho_i$ the Landau coupling depends on the wavenumber, the advection depends on $v_\perp$ and the compressive fluctuations act back on the Alfv\'enic ones \citep{Chandran2026}, so the distribution is no longer carried by a single flow, its streaming term no longer acts at each point separately, and the frozen-line solution does not hold in this form.
