\section{Frozen field lines}\label{sec:frozen}

S09 (\S6.3) showed that the kinetic equation of the compressive fluctuations becomes linear in the frame that moves with the $\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B$ flow. The flow carries the magnetic field lines with it, so in this frame the particles stream along field lines that do not move, and S09 concluded that the compressive fluctuations do not cascade along the moving field lines. \citet[\S\S2.6 and~4.1]{Kunz2015} made the same point for pressure-anisotropic plasmas, and \citet[\S2.3.1]{LithwickGoldreich2001} for a passive scalar in MHD turbulence. In this section we write the solution in this frame explicitly and say what it requires. We then use it to show that $W(m)$, and every other volume average that involves $G$ at one point only, evolves exactly as in linear phase mixing along the field lines at $t=0$.

\subsection{The kinetic equation in the frame of the flow}\label{fr:solution}

We consider one branch at a time and add to (\ref{eq:branch}) a collision operator $\mathcal C$ that acts on the velocity variable in the same way at every point, as the hypercollisions of section~\ref{sec:simulations} do:
\begin{equation}\label{lag:kin}
  \frac{\dd G}{\dd t}+s\,\gradpar(LG)=-\mathcal CG,\qquad \frac{\dd}{\dd t}=\partial_t+\{\Phi,\cdot\} .
\end{equation}
Here $G(\boldsymbol r,u,t)$ is the distribution of the branch. In a hierarchy truncated at $m=M$ the same steps apply to the vector $\boldsymbol g=(g_0,\dots,g_M)$ of its Hermite moments, with $LG$ replaced by $A\boldsymbol g$ (section~\ref{mod:hermite}).

Following S09, we change to Lagrangian variables, the initial positions $\boldsymbol\xi$ of the fluid elements of the $\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B$ flow. The element that starts at $\boldsymbol\xi$ is at time $t$ at the position
\begin{equation}\label{lag:map}
  \boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol X_t(\boldsymbol\xi)=\boldsymbol\xi+\int_0^t\uE\big(\boldsymbol X_{t'}(\boldsymbol\xi),t'\big)\,\dd t' ,
\end{equation}
and in these variables the advective derivative $\dd/\dd t$ in (\ref{lag:kin}) becomes $\partial_t$ at fixed $\boldsymbol\xi$. If the induction equation, the first of equations (\ref{eq:rmhd}), holds, the field lines are frozen into the flow and the direction of the field is given by Cauchy's solution (S09 eq.~215; \citealt[example~3]{Webb2014})
\begin{equation}\label{lag:cauchy}
  \bhat\big(\boldsymbol X_t(\boldsymbol\xi),t\big)=\bhat(\boldsymbol\xi,0)\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}\boldsymbol X_t(\boldsymbol\xi) .
\end{equation}
Cauchy's solution is usually written for $\boldsymbol B/\rho$. It holds for $\bhat=\zhat+\boldsymbol b_\perp$ because $\zhat\times\nabla_\perp$ of the induction equation is $\partial_t\bhat+\uE\cdot\nabla\bhat=\bhat\cdot\nabla\uE$, which is the ideal induction equation for $\boldsymbol B/\rho$ when $\rho$ and $B_z$ are uniform and the flow has no $z$ component. By the chain rule, the derivative along the field at time $t$ is then the derivative along the field lines at $t=0$, taken with respect to the labels (S09 eq.~216):
\begin{equation}\label{lag:intertwine}
  \gradpar=\bhat(\boldsymbol\xi,0)\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}\boldsymbol X_t\cdot\nabla=\bhat(\boldsymbol\xi,0)\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol\xi}\equiv\gradpar^{(0)} .
\end{equation}
An equivalent statement, which we use in section~\ref{fr:echoes}, is that the derivative along the field commutes with the advective derivative. By the Jacobi identity,
\begin{equation}\label{lag:commutator}
  \Big[\frac{\dd}{\dd t},\gradpar\Big]f=\{R,f\},\qquad R=\partial_t\Psi+\{\Phi,\Psi\}-\partial_z\Phi ,
\end{equation}
and $R=0$ by the first of equations (\ref{eq:rmhd}).

The operator $L$ and the collision operator act on the velocity dependence of $G$ at each point and contain no spatial derivatives, $L$ because the field relations of KRMHD are algebraic (section~\ref{mod:onebranch}). The change of variables leaves them as they are, and in the Lagrangian variables (\ref{lag:kin}) becomes
\begin{equation}\label{lag:linear}
  \partial_tG+s\,\gradpar^{(0)}(LG)=-\mathcal CG .
\end{equation}
This is the linear problem of phase mixing along the field lines frozen in their shape at $t=0$, with no flow, and its coefficients do not depend on time. If $h(\boldsymbol\xi,t)$ is its solution with $h(\boldsymbol\xi,0)=G(\boldsymbol\xi,0)$, then the solution of the full problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frozen}
  G\big(\boldsymbol X_t(\boldsymbol\xi),t\big)=h(\boldsymbol\xi,t) :
\end{equation}
the distribution phase mixes along the initial field lines exactly as in linear theory, and the flow only carries the result to new positions. For $\Lambda=\infty$ and without collisions, $L$ is multiplication by $u$, and $h$ is the ballistic response, the initial distribution at each $u$ moved by $sut$ along the initial field lines:
\begin{equation}\label{lag:ballistic}
  G\big(\boldsymbol X_t(\boldsymbol\xi),u,t\big)=G\big(\boldsymbol\xi_{-sut},u,0\big),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\xi_\ell$ is the point reached from $\boldsymbol\xi$ by following the field line of $\bhat(\cdot,0)$ through a distance $\ell$ in $z$. In a straight field this is the familiar $G(\boldsymbol\xi-sut\,\zhat,u,0)$. Equation (\ref{lag:ballistic}) makes exact the remark of S09 (\S6.2.4) that the ballistic response is also the nonlinear solution in Lagrangian variables.

The derivation uses three properties of the equations, and (\ref{eq:frozen}) holds as long as all three do. First, the induction equation must be ideal, with no resistivity and no forcing of $\Psi$, so that the field lines are frozen into the flow. The vorticity equation does not enter, and may be forced, viscous or even prescribed. Second, $G$ must have no source and no spatial dissipation, and its collisions must act on the velocity variable alone. Third, the fields must stay smooth, so that the trajectories (\ref{lag:map}) exist and the flow preserves volume. Whether solutions of the ideal three-dimensional RMHD equations stay smooth is not known in general, so the third property is an assumption about the time interval considered. Section~\ref{fr:breaks} lists the terms in our simulations that violate the first two.

\subsection{Single-point statistics}\label{fr:single}

We call a box average of the form
\begin{equation}\label{lag:Odef}
  \big\langle O\big(G,\gradpar G,\gradpar^2G,\dots\big)\big\rangle ,
\end{equation}
where $O$ is any function of the velocity dependence of $G$ and of a finite number of its derivatives along the field, all taken at the same point, a single-point statistic. Because the flow preserves volume, a box average has the same value whether it is computed over the positions $\boldsymbol r$ or over the labels $\boldsymbol\xi$, and by (\ref{lag:intertwine}) the derivatives along the field become derivatives along the initial field lines. A single-point statistic of $G$ at time $t$ is therefore equal to the same statistic of the linear solution $h$, computed with $\gradpar^{(0)}$. Every single-point statistic of $G$ thus evolves as in linear phase mixing along the field lines frozen at $t=0$, with no flow.

This covers $W(m,t)$, the correlations $\langle g_mg_n\rangle$, the collisional dissipation rate and the Hermite flux (\ref{eq:fluxCD}) computed with the full $\gradpar$. As long as the field lines stay frozen into the flow, the nonlinearity therefore moves no free energy between Hermite moments. It does not cover spectra in $k_\perp$ or $k_z$, or fluxes resolved in $k_\perp$ shells or computed with $\partial_z$ alone. These involve Fourier filters in $\boldsymbol r$, which combine the values of $G$ at different points and are changed by the relabelling.

\subsection{Forcing}\label{fr:forcing}

With a source on the right-hand side of (\ref{lag:kin}), the solution in the Lagrangian variables is the response to the initial condition plus the responses to the injections at all earlier times. Carried back to the positions, the free energy injected at a time $t'$ phase mixes along the field lines as they were at $t'$ and is then carried by the flow. If the forcing is white in time and independent of the Alfv\'enic fields, each injection is uncorrelated with everything that happened before it, including the free energy injected earlier and the shape of the field lines at the moment of injection. The cross terms between different injections then vanish on average, as for a linear Langevin equation \citep{Kanekar2015}, and the mean of any quadratic single-point statistic is the sum of the linear responses to the initial condition and to the individual injections. For the Hermite spectrum, on average over realisations of the forcing,
\begin{equation}\label{lag:meanforced}
  W(m,t)=W_0(m,t)+\int_0^tW_{t'}(m,t-t')\,\dd t' ,
\end{equation}
where $W_0(m,t)$ is the spectrum of the linear problem (\ref{lag:linear}) started from $G(0)$, and $W_{t'}(m,\vartheta)$ is the mean spectrum a time $\vartheta$ after an injection at $t'$, computed in the linear problem along the field lines frozen at $t'$, with an initial state that has the statistics of the forcing per unit time. This is a statement about the mean. A single realisation contains cross terms between injections at different times, and with forcing correlated in time the mean cross terms between injections closer together than the correlation time survive.

\subsection{Field-aligned wavenumbers}\label{fr:kpar}

In the Lagrangian variables the derivative along the field, $\gradpar^{(0)}$, does not depend on time. Neither, then, does the decomposition of $G$ into parts of definite field-aligned wavenumber $\kappa$, the eigenvalue of $-\ii\gradpar$.\footnote{Because $\nabla\cdot\bhat=0$, $-\ii\gradpar$ is Hermitian, so this decomposition exists, its parts are orthogonal and the $\kappa$ are real. In a straight field it is the Fourier decomposition in $z$, with $\kappa=k_z$. When the field lines fill a volume, $\kappa$ takes a continuum of values and the decomposition is an integral rather than a sum.} Each part phase mixes on its own, keeps its $\kappa$ and is carried by the flow. No part of $G$ therefore changes the sign of its field-aligned wavenumber. Without collisions the free energy at each field-aligned wavenumber is also constant in time, because the streaming at fixed $\kappa$ conserves the free energy (section~\ref{mod:hermite}) and the flow preserves volume. Section~\ref{sim:setup} measures this decomposition along traced field lines.

The Eulerian $k_z$ of the same part of $G$ can change sign. Consider the frozen-in shear $\Phi=-Szy$, $\Psi=-Sty$, an exact but not periodic solution of (\ref{eq:rmhd}): the flow $u_x=Sz$ tilts the field lines, $\gradpar=\partial_z+St\,\partial_x$. A Fourier mode of the labels becomes a Fourier mode of the positions with
\begin{equation}\label{lag:shear}
  k_z(t)=k_z(0)-Stk_x,\qquad \kappa=k_z(t)+Stk_x=k_z(0) .
\end{equation}
Its $k_z$ changes sign at $t=k_z(0)/(Sk_x)$ while its field-aligned wavenumber stays fixed. A diagnostic that sorts the free energy by the sign of $k_z$ counts the mode as reversed, although nothing has changed along the field.

\subsection{When the result fails}\label{fr:breaks}

Resistivity or hyperresistivity on $\Psi$, parallel dissipation of $z^\pm$, and forcing of $\Psi$ make $R$ in (\ref{lag:commutator}) non-zero, and the field lines are then not carried by the flow. Spatial dissipation of $G$, whether it depends on $k_\perp$ or on $k_z$, involves derivatives that do not become derivatives along the initial field lines in the Lagrangian variables, so (\ref{lag:linear}) acquires a term that depends on time. Fourier truncation of a simulation has the same effect, with an error that falls as the resolution increases (section~\ref{fr:check}). In the equations that GANDALF integrates, one hyperdissipation acts alike on $z^\pm$ and on every $g_m$, so it unfreezes the field lines and also dissipates $G$ in space. The hypercollisions act on the velocity variable only and are allowed. Forcing that adds the same increment to $z^+$ and $z^-$ forces $\Phi$ only and keeps the field lines frozen.

\subsection{Echoes}\label{fr:echoes}

While the field lines are frozen into the flow, $W(m,t)$ and the field-aligned Hermite flux evolve as in the linear problem (\ref{lag:linear}). A return of free energy to low $m$ must then already be present in the linear problem, as a linear echo of initial unmixing correlations, the recurrence of a truncated hierarchy, or slow phase mixing at small field-aligned wavenumbers; with white forcing the same holds for the mean of $W(m,t)$. In this sense ideal, smooth dynamics without spatial dissipation of $G$ produces no stochastic echoes in $W(m)$. In drift kinetics with a straight guide field \citep{Schekochihin2016,Parker2016} the particles stream along a fixed direction, $[\dd/\dd t,\partial_z]f=-\{\partial_z\Phi,f\}\neq0$, and the $z$-dependent flow couples positive and negative $k_z$, which is the mechanism of the stochastic echo; a flow independent of $z$ gives none \citetext{\citealp[appendix~B]{Schekochihin2016}; \citealp[\S5.2]{KanekarThesis2015}}. In KRMHD the shear $\partial_z\uE$ that would change the parallel wavenumber also bends the field lines together with $G$. With an ideal induction equation the commutator (\ref{lag:commutator}) vanishes, and no part of $G$ moves between positive and negative field-aligned wavenumbers (section~\ref{fr:kpar}), although its Eulerian $k_z$ can change sign (\ref{lag:shear}).

This does not contradict the simulations in which echoes were reported \citep{Meyrand2019}, which, like ours, dissipate both the magnetic field and the compressive distribution in space and so violate the first two conditions of section~\ref{fr:solution}. An echo in $W(m)$ requires such terms, or a failure of deterministic flux freezing for rough fields as the resistivity goes to zero, which the assumption of smooth fields excludes. \citet{Eyink2013} reported such a failure at high conductivity, and \citet[\S VIII.C]{Eyink2018} argued that in a turbulent inertial range the ideal Ohm's law does not imply flux freezing. The question for turbulence is therefore what the terms that break the frozen-line solution do to $W(m)$, which the model of section~\ref{sec:twochannel} addresses.
